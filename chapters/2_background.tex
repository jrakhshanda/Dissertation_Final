\chapter{Background \label{ch:background}}
\chaptertoc

This chapter presents the background needed to frame the research problem and the methods used in this thesis. It first reviews prior approaches to software architecture recovery and code-to-architecture mapping, with attention to how they represent and combine implementation-level evidence. It then introduces the artifact representations, graph-based learning methods, the graph properties that affect them, the learning paradigms, and the evaluation concepts used in the subsequent chapters.

\section{Prior Approaches \label{sec:bg_prior}}

Prior approaches to architecture recovery and C2A mapping draw on the same implementation signals: static dependencies between source files, directory layout, and identifiers and comments in the code. Some also use system version history, which is not studied in this thesis. The approaches differ in how they represent, combine, and learn from these signals. Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} gives selected examples, with the signals they use, their learning or aggregation paradigm, and a primary limitation; it is not an exhaustive inventory of the work discussed below. The remainder of this section traces the progression shown in the table, from search-based and clustering methods on dependency graphs, through hand-specified multi-signal fusion, to learned representations based on graph embeddings and language models.

\subsection{Software Architecture Recovery}

Traditional recovery methods treat software artifacts as a dependency graph and recover modules through several related paradigms. A first group formulates recovery as search over a modularization objective. Some methods optimize a single modularity criterion that favors high cohesion within modules and low coupling across them~\cite{bunch_sahc_nahc,dagc}, while others use multi-objective or evolutionary search to balance several criteria at once~\cite{mca_eca}. A second group clusters directly on dependency-derived similarities or information-theoretic criteria~\cite{cct,hcc_2019,limbo,wca}. ACDC~\cite{acdc} takes a different approach by building clusters from recurring subsystem patterns. A third group focuses on the clustering procedure itself. The fast clustering algorithm (FCA)~\cite{fca} speeds up clustering for large systems by working on the neighborhood degree matrix; Mohammadi and Izadkhah~\cite{neighborhood_tree_2019} build clusters from the neighborhoods of artifacts in the dependency graph, and Pourasghar et al.~\cite{gma_2021} use relationship depth. Overall, graph-based recovery methods can work well when dependencies reflect architectural structure, but their effectiveness depends on which dependency relations the graph includes and how the method uses them.

Semantic approaches use identifiers and comments instead of, or alongside, dependencies, and can outperform dependency-only methods in some systems~\cite{comparative_survey}. ARC~\cite{arc} uses latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) to infer concerns from source text and combines them with structural information. ZBR and Risi et al. derive semantic similarities using lexical zones and latent semantic indexing (LSI), respectively~\cite{zbr,risi2012fold}, while Kargar et al.~\cite{kargar2017semantic} build a semantic dependency graph from lexical cues and optimize it by local search. Anquetil et al.~\cite{file_clustering} take a related but distinct approach by clustering files according to their names, using common substrings and concepts found in routine names or comments. Non-structural signals of this kind can be noisy in isolation, since reused terminology may span modules.

Hybrid approaches use multiple sources to capture complementary evidence. Some integrate structural and semantic relations before hierarchical clustering~\cite{sc_misra,escrc}. Others incorporate semantic information into search-based modularization, and NSGA-III also uses development change history~\cite{sadat2019multi,nsga3}. Puchala et al.~\cite{ensemble_recovery} combine several clustering results into a consensus solution, while Yang et al.~\cite{evol} use semantic cues to guide label propagation over the dependency graph. Naim et al.~\cite{naim2017cchd} combine structural and contextual information through coordinated clustering, refining the combination through incremental developer feedback. SADE~\cite{sade} and SARIF~\cite{sarif} combine signals in weighted dependency graphs and apply community detection. SARIF also incorporates directory similarity. These methods bring information together at different stages, from constructing relations and defining search objectives to guiding or combining clustering results. In most cases, the fusion is specified by a designed procedure, consensus rule, or heuristic weighting rather than learned jointly as a representation of the signals.

Representation learning has also been applied to recovery. NEGAR~\cite{negar} learns node representations from dependency graphs through random walks and clusters the resulting vectors. It applies this pipeline separately to graphs at different implementation levels and does not incorporate non-structural signals.

Another approach uses large language models to infer architectural structures and patterns from source code. SemArc~\cite{sar_sem} uses LLMs to identify implementation-level and architectural semantics, drawing on canonical architectural patterns as a knowledge base. It also incorporates explicit and implicit dependencies before applying component-as-anchor clustering. Amalfitano et al.~\cite{amalfitano2025automated} evaluate general-purpose LLMs on identifying implementation-level class diagrams, architectural and design patterns, and architectural styles. Their prompts include a self-reflection step to simulate iterative refinement. ArchAgent~\cite{archagent} combines static analysis and adaptive code segmentation with LLM-based synthesis to recover multi-view architectures from large, cross-repository codebases.

\subsection{Code-to-Architecture Mapping}

Tzerpos and Holt~\cite{tzerpos1997orphan} introduced orphan adoption, the incremental assignment of unmapped entities to a documented architecture using naming and structural criteria. Christl et al.~\cite{christl2007automated} built on this with HuGMe, a semi-automatic method that extends a partial mapping through dependency-based attraction functions and leaves uncertain assignments for the architect to review. Bittencourt et al.~\cite{bittencourt_IR} later introduced the information-retrieval attraction functions IRAttract and LSIAttract, which use lexical similarity between entities and modules. InMap~\cite{InMap} takes an interactive recommendation approach, ranking candidate mappings from natural-language module descriptions. Like HuGMe, it keeps the architect involved, but through interactive recommendations rather than review of uncertain assignments. Together, these approaches rely on predefined criteria, attraction functions, or retrieval-based ranking rather than a jointly learned representation, the mapping analog of the fusion schemes above.

Learned classifiers provide another approach to mapping. Olsson et al.~\cite{olsson2022automatically} propose NBAttract, a semi-supervised Naive Bayes mapper that combines lexical and dependency information encoded via Concrete Dependency Abstraction (CDA). Florean et al.~\cite{florean2021textclassifierscomparison} compare text classifiers and text representations for the same task. An earlier study~\cite{jabeen2025graph} represents dependencies directly as edges in homogeneous and dependency-type-aware graphs, in contrast to NBAttract's feature-based encoding. It evaluates graph convolutional network (GCN) variants and uses iterative pseudo-labeling to expand a small initial labeled set.

Some mapping studies use pretrained embeddings as classifier inputs, while others prompt general-purpose LLMs to produce mappings. Johansson et al.~\cite{johansson2024mapping} evaluate pretrained embeddings alongside traditional representations such as TF-IDF as input to method-level classifiers. In follow-up work, Johansson and Caporuscio relate the performance of embedding-based mapping of functions and files to data properties, most consistently class separability~\cite{johansson2026mapping}. Rukmono et al.~\cite{rukmono2024deductive} use chain-of-thought prompting with GPT-4 to map implementation units to a predefined reference architecture. Both approaches use lexical or semantic information, while typed dependencies remain implicit in their inputs.

\subsection{Limitations Across Recovery and Mapping}

Across recovery and mapping, many of the reviewed approaches rely on a single information source, combine sources through predefined rules, or use a fixed representation of each source within a learned model. Comparing the contributions of graph structure, directory organization, and source-code content requires varying these choices under matched conditions. The studies comprising this thesis examine how these signals can be represented, combined, and propagated for architecture recovery and C2A mapping.

\section[Representing Artifact Content]{Representing Artifact Content and Organization \label{sec:bg_content}}

A software system carries architectural evidence in more than one form. Its files and members are connected by static dependencies, but they also have names, occupy locations in the folder hierarchy that reflect how developers group related functionality~\cite{garcia2013}, and contain identifiers and comments that encode domain-specific knowledge~\cite{sarif}. In a representation learning framework, dependencies can be represented as graph relations, while the content associated with individual artifacts must be encoded numerically for a learning model to operate on. This section introduces the two families of representation used in the thesis: lexical and contextual.


\subsection{Lexical Representations \label{sec:bg_lexical}}

Lexical representations treat folder-path components and source-code identifiers as tokens. The simplest representation is a binary bag-of-words (BoW) vector, in which each dimension indicates whether a token is present in the artifact’s folder path or source code. Because it allocates one dimension to every vocabulary token, the representation becomes high-dimensional and sparse as the vocabulary grows. Word2Vec (W2V)~\cite{word2vec} instead learns a dense vector for each source-code identifier from a corpus of source-code tokens using a skip-gram model.

In skip-gram, each token is used to predict tokens occurring within a fixed context window. The vectors are trained to assign higher scores to observed token--context pairs than to randomly sampled pairs through negative sampling. As a result, tokens that occur in similar contexts receive similar vectors. An artifact’s Word2Vec representation is obtained by averaging the vectors of its source-code identifiers, producing a compact, fixed-dimensional encoding.

\subsection{Contextual Representations}

Contextual representations encode tokens in the context of the surrounding code rather than as an unordered collection of identifiers. The same identifier can therefore receive different representations in different contexts, whereas a trained Word2Vec model assigns a fixed vector to each token. Transformer-based encoders use attention to combine information from tokens within the input sequence, allowing each token’s representation to reflect how it is used in the code. Through pretraining on large corpora, these encoders learn patterns that can support different code analysis tasks. UniXcoder~\cite{unixcoder} is one such model, producing contextual token representations that can be pooled into a single vector for a code fragment.

A pretrained code encoder can be used without further training or adapted through fine-tuning. Fine-tuning adjusts its representations using a training objective and examples relevant to the target setting, which can include dependency information.

\section[Graph Representation Learning]{Graph-Based Representation Learning \label{sec:bg-gnn}}

Content encoders process artifacts individually, although their representations can capture dependency information introduced during fine-tuning. Graph-based representation learning uses the dependencies and containment relations between artifacts explicitly to learn node representations. The approaches used in this thesis fall into two groups: random-walk embeddings, which derive vectors directly from graph neighborhoods, and graph neural networks, which propagate and transform information along graph edges.

\subsection{Random-Walk Embeddings \label{sec:bg_random_walk}}
Random-walk methods learn node embeddings in two steps. First, they construct a corpus by sampling random walks over the graph, where each walk is a sequence of nodes. Node2Vec (N2V)~\cite{node2vec} samples walks over a homogeneous graph, which in this thesis is unweighted and undirected, whereas MetaPath2Vec (M2V)~\cite{metapath2vec} extends this to heterogeneous graphs by constraining walks to follow a predefined sequence of node and relation types. Second, a skip-gram model (see Section~\ref{sec:bg_lexical}) is trained over this corpus, treating the nodes within a fixed context window of a target node as its observed context, so that nodes with similar walk contexts receive similar embeddings. Walk length, context window, and the number of walks per node determine which nodes are treated as similar. These methods learn from walk co-occurrence alone and therefore do not jointly incorporate artifact content.

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks \label{sec:bg_gnns}}
Graph neural networks (GNNs) provide a common framework for learning from artifact-level information and graph structure, producing node representations shaped by both. A GNN encoder maps each node, together with its initial representation and graph neighborhood, to a vector through message passing~\cite{neuralMP}. At each layer, a node aggregates the current representations of its neighbors, combines them with its own, and applies a learned transformation. GNN architectures differ primarily in how they transform and aggregate these messages, and this thesis uses three variants.

The graph convolutional network~\cite{gcn} aggregates neighboring representations through degree-normalized message passing, which helps prevent high-degree nodes from dominating solely because they have many dependencies. The graph attention network (GAT)~\cite{gat} instead learns attention weights over neighbors, allowing their contributions to differ according to their representations. Both encoders can be applied either to a graph in which dependency types are collapsed or to a multi-relational graph with relation-specific parameters. When the graph contains multiple node types and typed relations, this thesis also uses the heterogeneous graph transformer (HGT)~\cite{hgt}, which parameterizes attention by meta-relation. Thus, the contribution of a neighbor depends jointly on the source-node type, target-node type, and edge type.
\subsection{Graph Properties and Learning \label{sec:bg_properties}}

Two properties are particularly relevant to how well graph-based encoders can support downstream tasks: homophily and structural reach. A graph is homophilic with respect to a labeling when connected nodes tend to share a label. Under homophily, neighborhood aggregation reinforces label-consistent information; under heterophily, it mixes information from different labels and may obscure distinctions needed by the downstream task~\cite{gnn_homophily}. Formally, let $G=(V,E)$ be a graph in which each node $v \in V$ has a label $y_v$. Homophily $\mathcal{H}$ is the proportion of edges whose two endpoints have the same label,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H} = \frac{|\{e=(u,v) \in E : y_u = y_v\}|}{|E|},
\label{eq:homophily}
\end{equation}
so that $\mathcal{H}=0$ means every edge connects nodes with different labels and $\mathcal{H}=1$ means every edge connects nodes with the same label~\cite{gnn_homophily}. For software systems, the nodes are files, the edges are static dependencies between them merged across dependency types, and the label is the ground-truth module. In this setting, homophily relates to cohesion and coupling by measuring the proportion of dependencies that remain within modules rather than cross module boundaries. It provides an overall measure of this balance, but does not distinguish how those dependencies are distributed across individual modules.

Since $\mathcal{H}$ is computed from the reference decomposition, it can be determined only once such a decomposition exists. The concentration of dependencies within modules varies across systems, and so does the usefulness of dependency-driven random walks and message passing.

The second property is the structural reach of the encoder, which we use to mean the set of nodes that can contribute information to a node representation. For an $L$-layer message-passing encoder, this is the node's $L$-hop neighborhood. Reach describes which nodes can contribute by construction, rather than how much they contribute in practice. Increasing depth extends this reach, but repeated aggregation can make node representations progressively less distinguishable, an effect known as over-smoothing~\cite{gnn_oversmoothing}. GNN encoders are therefore commonly kept shallow.

With a shallow encoder, access to more distant architectural information depends on how the input graph connects the nodes. Changing these connections can make that information reachable within fewer message-passing steps. Random-walk embeddings can capture longer-range structural context through sampled walks, although they do not propagate artifact-level representations along the graph.

Two further techniques alter the structural information available to a shallow encoder without increasing its number of message-passing layers. Laplacian positional encoding (LPE) augments each node representation with leading eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, giving the encoder information about the node's structural position before message passing begins~\cite{lpe}. Graph diffusion convolution (GDC)~\cite{gdc} rewires the input graph using personalized PageRank (PPR), connecting diffusion-close nodes directly and assigning the resulting edges diffusion-derived weights. This makes multi-hop structural proximity available within a fixed number of message-passing layers.

\subsection{Learning Paradigms \label{sec:bg_learning}}

The encoder architecture determines how information propagates through the graph, but not what drives learning. That role belongs to the training objective, which depends on the supervision available. The studies in this thesis use two learning settings that reflect the recovery and mapping tasks.

\paragraph{Architecture recovery.} No module labels are available during recovery, so the studies use self-supervised learning, in which the model learns from an auxiliary signal derived from the graph itself. These methods construct positive and negative training examples from the graph, with the examples differing between methods. Binary cross-entropy (BCE) measures how well the model distinguishes positive from negative examples. For a single example, it is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}} = -\left[y\log\hat{y} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})\right],
    \label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}
where $y \in \{0,1\}$ is the target and $\hat{y}$ is the predicted probability.

Contrastive learning trains representations by contrasting positive and negative pairs. Deep Graph Infomax (DGI)~\cite{dgi} applies this principle to graphs by treating node--summary pairs in the original graph as positive and pairs constructed from a corrupted view as negative. Graph autoencoders (GAE)~\cite{gae} instead learn node representations by reconstructing observed graph edges, treating observed edges as positives and sampled non-edges as negatives. Their variational form (VGAE)~\cite{gae} models each node's latent representation as a Gaussian distribution rather than a single vector and adds a Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence term that penalizes deviations from a standard normal prior. Random-walk methods learn without module labels through the skip-gram objective described in Section~\ref{sec:bg_random_walk}.

The learned embeddings are then partitioned with a clustering algorithm, typically agglomerative hierarchical clustering~\cite{agglomerative}, for architecture recovery. This clustering step is unsupervised: it derives module groups from similarity between embeddings. The number of clusters is either selected automatically from a predefined search range or fixed in advance.

\paragraph{Code-to-architecture mapping.} Mapping starts from known module assignments for a limited number of files, so the studies train a classifier on these labeled files with supervised learning. Because the module set is known and fixed, the classifier assigns each file to one of the $K$ predefined modules through a softmax layer, which yields a probability distribution over the modules, and the highest softmax probability of a prediction is its confidence. It is trained with categorical cross-entropy (CCE), $-\sum_{k=1}^{K} y_k\log\hat{y}_k$, which for a one-hot target is the negative log-probability assigned to the true module.

Unlabeled files can also contribute through iterative self-training~\cite{lee2013pseudo}, which Paper~III applies on top of supervised training. The classifier assigns pseudo-labels to unlabeled files whose confidence exceeds a predefined threshold, retrains on the labeled and pseudo-labeled files, and repeats over successive rounds until no prediction meets the criterion or a maximum number of rounds is reached.

\section[Evaluation and Ground Truth]{Evaluation and Ground Truth}
\label{sec:bg-eval}

The studies in this thesis evaluate their outputs against reference decompositions from published, expert-validated benchmarks, supporting comparison with prior work. Recovery produces a partition whose cluster labels have no predefined meaning, whereas mapping assigns files to known architectural modules. Recovery is therefore evaluated through partition-agreement metrics and mapping through conventional classification metrics. This section introduces these measures and discusses how reference decompositions should be understood as ground truth. The provenance of the specific reference mappings is discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

A reference decomposition provides an established basis for comparison, but it need not be the only reasonable architectural interpretation of a system. Experts may disagree on architectural boundaries and design intent~\cite{garcia2013}, so a recovered decomposition can differ from the reference without necessarily being wrong.

Complementary metrics assess different aspects of a recovered decomposition. MoJoFM, A2A, and \cc{} measure alignment with a reference decomposition. The adjusted Rand index (ARI)~\cite{ari} measures partition agreement while correcting for chance. TurboMQ provides a complementary, reference-independent measure of modularization quality based on the dependency graph.

MoJoFM~\cite{mojofm} is widely used in architecture recovery. It counts the minimum number of move and join operations needed to transform the recovered partition into the reference partition, normalized by the maximum possible number of such operations. Because its join operations are comparatively cheap, MoJoFM is less sensitive to over-fragmentation: many small clusters can still score well when they broadly align with the reference. A2A~\cite{a2a} extends MoJoFM with costs for adding, removing, and moving entities and clusters. It can assign a high score to two architectures that are structurally quite different, overestimating their similarity~\cite{turbomq}.

\cc{}~\cite{c2c} measures the percentage of reference modules that are sufficiently covered by a recovered module, where coverage is judged against a threshold on the overlap between the two; conventional thresholds of 0.10, 0.33, and 0.50 correspond to weak, moderate, and majority matches. This makes it sensitive to fragmentation: a reference module split across several recovered modules does not count as matched if none of them meets the chosen overlap threshold, even where individual file assignments look reasonable under a different metric. ARI ranges from $-1$ to $1$, where $0$ is the agreement expected by chance.

Unlike the metrics above, which measure alignment with the reference decomposition, TurboMQ~\cite{turbomq} measures modularity: it rewards intra-cluster cohesion and penalizes inter-cluster coupling, computed directly on the dependency graph without reference to any external partition. TurboMQ can favor decompositions with many clusters. Normalized TurboMQ (\tmq{}) divides the TurboMQ score by the number of recovered clusters to reduce this bias.

The reference-agreement metrics described above assess how closely a recovered decomposition matches an expert-produced reference, whereas TurboMQ evaluates its dependency structure. Expert-produced references can reflect responsibilities and project organization identified through documentation, source code, and knowledge of the system. These boundaries need not coincide with groups of densely connected files, since shared utilities and libraries can create dependencies across architectural modules~\cite{garcia2013}. A reference decomposition therefore need not maximize dependency modularity, and a recovered decomposition can score well on one kind of measure without scoring well on the other.

For mapping, the evaluation is a per-file classification into known modules, and the standard metrics apply directly. Precision, recall, and their harmonic mean F1 are reported per module and aggregated at the macro and micro level. The macro-average treats every module equally and therefore surfaces performance on small modules that would be hidden by a micro-average dominated by large ones. Reporting both reveals differences in performance across modules of different sizes.
